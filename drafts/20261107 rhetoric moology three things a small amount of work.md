### what does 三件事 微小的工作 mean

**"三件事" (Three things)** and **"微小的工作" (A small amount of work)** is a viral Chinese internet meme originating from a famous 2009 video of Jiang Zemin, the former General Secretary of the Chinese Communist Party (CCP). [](https://news.qq.com/rain/a/20221206A04DHL00)

When translated literally, it means **"Three things, \[just\] a small amount of work."** In contemporary internet culture, however, it is used as a self-deprecating or ironic expression to describe a monumental, historic achievement while pretending it was "no big deal."

---

#### 🏛️ The Origin Story

The quote comes from an informal speech Jiang Zemin gave during a visit to the China Machinery Academy in 2009\. He was reflecting on his unexpected rise to the top leadership of China in 1989 and his tenure through 2002.

In the viral clip, he humbly remarked:

> _"I have spent over ten years in Beijing, and I haven't done much else besides **three things (三件事)**..."_

He then listed three massive, country-altering milestones: [](https://news.qq.com/rain/a/20221206A04DHL00)

1. Establishing China's **socialist market economy**.
2. Writing **Deng Xiaoping Theory** into the party constitution.
3. Formulating the **"Three Represents"** ideology.

He concluded his reflection by saying:

> _"If there's any other achievement, it's the banning of military commercial businesses... But these are all secondary. I am deeply ashamed that **I’ve only done a small amount of work (微小的工作)**."_

#### 💬 How the Meme is Used Today

Chinese netizens love using these phrases in everyday contexts to achieve a comedic effect known as **"humblebragging"** or mock-modesty. It is typically structured to state a massive achievement followed by a dismissive conclusion.

* **Example 1 (Gaming):** _"I carried the team, wiped out the entire enemy squad, and took the objective. I am ashamed to say I only did a small amount of work."_
* **Example 2 (Academics):** _"I wrote three major research papers, got a perfect GPA, and passed my finals. Just three things, a small amount of work."_
* **Example 3 (Workplace):** _"I stayed up all night, fixed the server crash, and saved the company's biggest account. Just a bit of minor work."_

The phrase belongs to a broader online subculture in China known as **"Moology" (膜蛤文化)**, which fondly satirizes Jiang Zemin's unique personal style, eccentric quotes, and historic speeches.

### What are the milestones about

The three milestones Jiang Zemin mentioned represent the massive economic, political, and military transformations that shaped modern China during the 1990s and early 2000s:

#### 1\. Establishing the Socialist Market Economy (社会主义市场经济)

* **What it means:** This was the official pivot that allowed China to blend a communist political system with a capitalist market system.
* **The impact:** Prior to this, China's economy was strictly state-controlled. By legally adopting the "socialist market economy" framework at the 14th National Congress in 1992, China opened its doors wider to private enterprise, foreign investment, and global trade, directly paving the way for its boom into a global economic superpower.

#### 2\. Writing Deng Xiaoping Theory into the CCP Constitution (邓小平理论)

* **What it means:** Deng Xiaoping was the architect of China's economic reforms, famously known for the philosophy _"It doesn't matter if a cat is black or white, so long as it catches mice."_
* **The impact:** By officially enshrining Deng's pragmatic, economy-first philosophy into the party's constitution in 1997, Jiang cemented a permanent shift away from the rigid, ideological class struggles of the Mao Zedong era. It ensured that China would stay firmly on the path of modernization and openness.

#### 3\. Formulating the "Three Represents" Ideology (三个代表)

* **What it means:** This was Jiang Zemin's own political ideology, introduced in 2000\. It stated that the Communist Party must represent advanced productive forces, advanced culture, and the fundamental interests of the overwhelming majority of the Chinese people.
* **The impact:** In plain terms, this was a massive political shift that **allowed private entrepreneurs and billionaires to join the Communist Party**. Previously, capitalists were viewed as enemies of a worker-led communist state. This ideology formally invited China's new wealthy class into the political tent to ensure the party remained relevant.

---

#### 🎖️ The "Secondary" Milestone: Banning Military Businesses

Though not part of his main "three things," Jiang also proudly mentioned banning the military from running commercial businesses (_军队一律不得经商_).

During the 1980s and 90s, the People's Liberation Army (PLA) ran an enormous commercial empire, owning everything from hotels and factories to nightclubs and shipping companies. This led to rampant corruption and distracted from national defense. In 1998, Jiang ordered the military to completely divest from these businesses, a highly dangerous and monumental political move that successfully forced the military back into being a professional fighting force.

## What is moology about, why is it called

### What is moology about, why is it called

**Moology** (known in Chinese as **Moha Culture / 膜蛤文化**) is a prominent Chinese internet subculture centered around playfully obsessing over, quoting, and memeing former leader Jiang Zemin. [](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moha%5F%28meme%29)

#### 🐸 Why is it called "Moology"?

The English term **"Moology"** is a translation-play on the Chinese word **_Móhá_ (膜蛤)**: [](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moha%5F%28meme%29)

* **Mó (膜):** Short for _móbài_ (膜拜), which means "to worship" or "to passionately admire".
* **Há (蛤):** Short for _hámó_ (蛤蟆), which means **toad**. [](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/325864518%5FTurning%5Fa%5Fcommunist%5Fparty%5Fleader%5Finto%5Fan%5Finternet%5Fmeme%5Fthe%5Fpolitical%5Fand%5Fapolitical%5Faspects%5Fof%5FChina's%5Ftoad%5Fworship%5Fculture)

Put together, it literally translates to **"Toad Worship"**. [](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moha%5F%28meme%29)

Netizens began mockingly nicknaming Jiang Zemin "The Toad" because of his distinct physical appearance—most notably his massive, thick, square-rimmed black glasses, wide mouth, and his habit of wearing his trousers pulled up exceptionally high past his waist. Members of this internet subculture refer to themselves as **"Toad Fans"** (_Hásī_ / 蛤丝) or **"Mogicians"** (_Mófǎshī_ / 膜法师, a wordplay on "magician" and "worshiping the toad"). [](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/325864518%5FTurning%5Fa%5Fcommunist%5Fparty%5Fleader%5Finto%5Fan%5Finternet%5Fmeme%5Fthe%5Fpolitical%5Fand%5Fapolitical%5Faspects%5Fof%5FChina's%5Ftoad%5Fworship%5Fculture)

#### 🎬 What is Moology Actually About?

Moology is essentially an extensive ecosystem of inside jokes built entirely out of Jiang Zemin's historical speeches, interviews, and public appearances. [](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moha%5F%28meme%29)

Unlike many highly rehearsed, strictly choreographed modern politicians, Jiang was famously expressive, eccentric, and prone to breaking protocol. He would abruptly burst into song, recite foreign poetry, or comb his hair in front of international cameras. Netizens obsessively study a core set of viral videos to pull quotes, templates, and running jokes: [](https://www.thechinastory.org/yearbooks/yearbook-2015/chapter-4-wood-%E6%9C%A8-the-crystal-clear-waters-of-the-chinese-internet/worshipping-jiang-zemins-toad/)

1. **The Hong Kong Journalist Incident (2000):** This is the holy grail of Moology. Angered by a sharp question from Hong Kong journalist Sharon Cheung, Jiang broke into a mix of Mandarin, Cantonese, and English, scolding the press corps for being **"too young, too simple, sometimes naive."** These phrases became legendary catchphrases across the Chinese internet. [](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moha%5F%28meme%29)
2. **The Mike Wallace Interview (2000):** Jiang sat down for a famous, candid interview on CBS's _60 Minutes_ with American journalist Mike Wallace. Netizens love quoting Jiang's relaxed banter, where he proudly joked about being much more open than westerners gave him credit for. [](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moha%5F%28meme%29)
3. **The "Three Things" Speech (2009):** The origin of the phrases mentioned earlier ("three things," "a small amount of work"). [](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moha%5F%28meme%29)

#### 🔄 The Political Shift: From Mockery to Nostalgia

Moology has undergone a fascinating cultural evolution since its inception around 2014: [](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/325864518%5FTurning%5Fa%5Fcommunist%5Fparty%5Fleader%5Finto%5Fan%5Finternet%5Fmeme%5Fthe%5Fpolitical%5Fand%5Fapolitical%5Faspects%5Fof%5FChina's%5Ftoad%5Fworship%5Fculture)

* **Phase 1: Ridicule.** Early on, the "toad" nickname and memes were used by critics to mock Jiang's appearance and his dramatic outbursts.
* **Phase 2: Affectionate Nostalgia.** Over time, the tone completely flipped into a form of ironic adoration. Younger generations (especially millennials and Gen Z) began to look back at Jiang’s era as a time of incredible economic growth, dynamic opening to the world, and relatively relaxed media freedom compared to modern tight internet controls. [](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-asia-china-63813122)

Today, using Moology memes is a way for young people to build a shared online community, bypass heavy keyword censorship by using frog and toad emojis, and quietly signal a longing for a more expressive, colorful political past. [](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/1369118X.2018.1485722)

## Why do they behave this way unlike other leaders

### Why do they behave this way unlike other leaders

Jiang Zemin’s highly expressive, eccentric, and colorful behavior stands in stark contrast to the stiff, intensely rehearsed "robot-like" demeanor of modern Chinese leaders. [](https://www.chinafile.com/library/nyrb-china-archive/jiang-zemin-mystery)

Henry Kissinger once noted that Jiang was "the least Middle Kingdom-type of personality" he had encountered, behaving more like a warm, informal family member. There are a few core reasons why his political style was so vastly different: 

#### 🎓 1\. An Elite, Pre-Revolutionary Education

Unlike later leaders who grew up entirely under the rigid, isolated cultural environment of Maoist China, Jiang Zemin was born in 1926 and received a cosmopolitan, western-style education before the Communist Party took power in 1949. [](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jiang%5FZemin)

* **The Polyglot:** He was a true linguistic chameleon. He studied in English during his youth and went on to learn **fluent Russian, Romanian, English, and passable German and Japanese**. He felt entirely comfortable abandoning translators to joke or debate directly with foreign leaders and journalists. [](https://www.oneroinstitute.org/research/jiang-zemin-accomplishments-shortcomings)
* **The Artist:** He was deeply trained in both arts and sciences. Jiang was a trained electrical engineer but also a classical music enthusiast who played the piano, the flute, and the traditional _erhu_. He loved showing off these skills—famously singing "Love Me Tender" at global summits or reciting the Gettysburg Address in English to visiting delegates. [](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Jiang-Zemin)

#### 🤝 2\. The Urgent Need to Mend China’s Global Image

Jiang unexpectedly became the top leader in 1989, immediately following the Tiananmen Square protests. At the time, China was globally isolated, facing severe Western sanctions, and heavily distrusted on the world stage. [](https://globalpolitics.in/view%5Fnotes.php?recordNo=683&url=Jiang%20Zemin%20and%20his%20Legacies)

Jiang recognized that a rigid, doctrinaire communist posture would keep China isolated. He used his colorful, extroverted personality as a deliberate diplomatic tool to humanize China. By bantering about sensitive topics on live TV with Bill Clinton or locking horns with Mike Wallace on _60 Minutes_, he signaled to the West that China’s leadership was confident, open-minded, and ready to do business. [](https://www.thinkchina.sg/politics/jiang-zemin-chinese-leader-whose-achievements-outweighed-shortcomings)

#### 🏛️ 3\. A Shift to Collective Leadership

During Jiang's tenure, the CCP was operating under a system of **collective leadership** established by Deng Xiaoping. No single leader had absolute, unchecked power like Mao Zedong did in the past, or like Xi Jinping does today.

Because power was shared among a committee, the political atmosphere within the elite circles was relatively relaxed. Leaders were allowed to display personal quirks, distinct characters, and individual styles without it being viewed as a threat to party discipline or an unauthorized deviation from a singular party line. [](https://www.thinkchina.sg/politics/jiang-zemin-chinese-leader-whose-achievements-outweighed-shortcomings)

#### 🛡️ Why Modern Leaders Don't Act This Way

Following Jiang Zemin, Chinese political culture underwent a massive shift toward uniformity:

* **The Risk of Looking "Weak":** Under subsequent administrations, spontaneity began to be viewed by the party as a vulnerability. An unexpected outburst or a joke could be micro-analyzed by foreign media or weaponized by domestic political rivals.
* **The Era of Total Discipline:** Modern Chinese political aesthetics prioritize absolute control, discipline, and gravitas. Speeches are strictly vetted, and leaders maintain a characteristically undemonstrative, stoic appearance to project unshakeable state power.

To young Chinese citizens today, Jiang Zemin feels like a character from a completely different universe—a politician who was undeniably flawed and politically tough, but uniquely human, highly capable, and completely unapologetic about who he was. [](https://www.bbc.com/news/world-asia-14041962)

### Why did they become the leader

Jiang Zemin became the leader of China purely by accident. In early 1989, he was on the verge of retirement from his post as the Party Secretary of Shanghai and had no expectations of rising to national power. [](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jiang%5FZemin)

His sudden ascent to the top was a direct result of the **1989 Tiananmen Square crisis** and an intense political deadlock among the ruling elite. [](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/chinese-top-leadership-changes-jiang-zemin-takes-party-chair/)

#### The Ouster of Zhao Ziyang

Before the crisis, the General Secretary of the Chinese Communist Party was Zhao Ziyang. When the student-led pro-democracy protests erupted in Beijing, Zhao advocated for a conciliatory, soft-line approach to negotiate with the students. [](https://www.asahi.com/ajw/articles/14781390)

The paramount leader, Deng Xiaoping, and the conservative party elders fiercely disagreed, favoring a hardline military crackdown. Because of his sympathy toward the protesters, Zhao was stripped of his power, purged from the leadership, and placed under house arrest for the rest of his life. [](https://theconversation.com/jiang-zemin-propelled-chinas-economic-rise-in-the-world-leaving-his-successors-to-deal-with-the-massive-inequality-that-followed-195675)

#### How Jiang Stood Out in Shanghai

While Beijing was descending into chaos, similar student protests broke out in Shanghai. As the mayor and party boss of Shanghai, Jiang handled the situation in a way that caught Deng Xiaoping's eye: [](https://www.oneroinstitute.org/research/jiang-zemin-accomplishments-shortcomings)

* **He was firm but avoided bloodshed:** Jiang successfully de-escalated the Shanghai protests without relying on military force.
* **He pleased the conservatives:** He swiftly shut down a liberal newspaper, the _World Economic Herald_, which he deemed a threat to stability. This proved his absolute loyalty to party control.
* **He pleased the reformers:** He was an engineer and a competent administrator who supported open-market economic modernization. [](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Jiang-Zemin)

#### The Ultimate Compromise Candidate

Following the bloody June 4th crackdown in Tiananmen Square, the top levels of the CCP were deeply fractured. The party elders needed a replacement for Zhao Ziyang who could unite the warring factions. [](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jiang%5FZemin)

The staunch conservatives wanted a leader who would clamp down on political dissent, while the liberal reformers wanted someone who would keep opening up the economy. [](https://www.oneroinstitute.org/research/jiang-zemin-accomplishments-shortcomings)

Because Jiang Zemin was not part of any faction in Beijing, he was chosen as the **perfect compromise candidate**. Deng Xiaoping personally drafted Jiang to move to Beijing and take the reins of the party. Though initially viewed as a temporary transitional figure who wouldn't last long, Jiang successfully consolidated his power and went on to rule China for over a decade. [](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jiang%5FZemin)

### How was the top levels fractured

The fractures at the top level of the Chinese Communist Party in 1989 were deeply ideological and highly personal. The leadership wasn't just split into regular political disagreements—it was divided down the middle over **the very future of China**.

The core divide separated two major camps, with the military playing a powerful wild-card role.

#### 1\. The Economic Reformers (The Liberals)

Led by the ousted General Secretary **Zhao Ziyang** and his predecessor **Hu Yaobang**, this group believed that China’s rapid economic opening should be accompanied by structural changes, political flexibility, and a lighter government touch.

When the student protests began, this faction viewed the youth not as counter-revolutionaries, but as patriotic citizens concerned about corruption and inflation. They wanted to negotiate, implement anti-corruption laws, and grant the press more freedom to reporting on governance.

#### 2\. The Hardline Conservatives (The Traditionalists)

Led by Premier **Li Peng** and heavily backed by powerful party elders like Chen Yun, this faction was terrified that market reforms were spinning out of control. They believed that looseness in political control would lead to chaos (_luan_) and destroy the Communist Party.

They viewed the Tiananmen protests as a Western-backed plot to overthrow the government. They wanted to halt aggressive economic reforms, reinstitute strict price controls, and crush the protests using total state power to re-establish stability.

#### 3\. The Military Faction (The Yang Brothers)

The People’s Liberation Army (PLA) was itself deeply conflicted about being ordered to turn its weapons against Chinese civilians. Within the high command, a massive power center was controlled by **Yang Shangkun** (the President of China) and his half-brother **Yang Baibing** (a top general). [](http://www.bjreview.com/90th/2011-03/30/content%5F357540.htm)

Initially, Yang Shangkun hesitated to use force and sympathetic elements in the military openly opposed martial law. However, once paramount leader Deng Xiaoping commanded obedience, the Yang brothers enforced the crackdown. This created an incredibly dangerous dynamic: the military held the ultimate keys to power, and the "Yang Family Clique" became so powerful that they threatened the authority of any civilian leader—including Jiang Zemin.

#### Why Jiang Zemin Was Caught in the Middle

When Deng Xiaoping dropped Jiang Zemin into Beijing as the new chief, Jiang had a completely fractured hand to play:

* The **reformers** hated him because his predecessor (Zhao Ziyang) had been unfairly purged.
* The **conservatives** distrusted him because he was a pro-market engineer from Shanghai who might continue the capitalist policies they opposed.
* The **military** didn't respect him because he had absolutely zero military experience and was viewed as a soft bureaucrat.

It took Jiang Zemin nearly three years of careful political maneuvering—alongside Deng Xiaoping’s famous 1992 Southern Tour—to finally outmaneuver the hardline economic conservatives and systematically strip the Yang family of their military control, finally solidifying his grasp over the party. [](https://www.idcpc.org.cn/english2023/tjzl/cpcjj/PartyCongresses/202307/t20230727%5F157826.html)

## Why did the reformers or conservatives think this way, why did the hard-line supporters win

### Why did the reformers or conservatives think this way, why did the hard-line supporters win

To understand why each side thought the way they did, you have to look at the massive trauma China had experienced just years prior. The two sides were looking at completely different history books to justify their actions.

#### Why the Reformers Thought This Way

The Reformers (like Zhao Ziyang) believed that **economic growth without political openness was a ticking time bomb.**

* **The Problem:** By opening up the economy but keeping a closed, rigid political system, China had inadvertently created massive inflation and rampant corruption. Government officials were buying goods at cheap state-controlled prices and selling them for massive profits on the new free market.
* **The Solution:** The reformers believed the student protesters were right to be angry about corruption. They argued that the only way to save China was to modernize the government alongside the economy—by allowing more press freedom, rule of law, and transparency so the public could help keep corrupt officials in check.

#### Why the Conservatives Thought This Way

The Conservatives (like Li Peng) believed that **political openness would plunge China straight back into bloody chaos.**

* **The Trauma:** The older generation of leaders had survived the **Cultural Revolution (1966–1976)**, a decade of absolute anarchy where student-led groups ("Red Guards") tore society apart, overthrew local governments, and dragged officials through the streets.
* **The Fear:** When they saw hundreds of thousands of students occupying Tiananmen Square, shouting slogans and building replicas of the Statue of Liberty, the elders did not see "democracy"—they saw the terrifying return of lawless mob rule. They genuinely believed that if they gave an inch, the country would collapse into a civil war, destroying the economic progress they had just made.

---

#### Why the Hardliners Won

The hardliners did not win because their arguments were better; they won because **they had the backing of the ultimate veto power: Deng Xiaoping.**

While Deng Xiaoping loved economic reform, he was an old-school revolutionary soldier who believed in absolute, top-down Communist Party discipline. He was the "Paramount Leader," meaning that even though he held no official top titles at the time, his word was final law.

The hardline victory came down to three turning points:

1. **The "Western Plot" Argument:** Hardliners successfully convinced Deng Xiaoping that the protests were no longer a domestic student movement, but a Western-backed "counter-revolutionary rebellion" designed to overthrow communist rule entirely. This triggered Deng’s deepest fears about foreign intervention.
2. **The Purge of Zhao Ziyang:** Zhao Ziyang made a desperate, unauthorized speech to the students in the square, weeping and telling them, "We came too late." The hardliners used this to convince Deng that Zhao was breaking party unity and trying to split the CCP from within. Deng immediately stripped Zhao of all power.
3. **Control of the Military:** Once Zhao Ziyang was removed, the hardliners had an open path to declare Martial Law. Because Deng Xiaoping still chaired the Central Military Commission, the army obeyed his command to march into Beijing on June 4, 1989, crushing the protests through overwhelming force.

By utilizing total military power, the hardliners successfully froze all political reform in China for decades to come, setting the stage for Jiang Zemin to inherit a tightly controlled, highly disciplined state.

### How did they handle the situation in Shanghai

Jiang Zemin’s handling of the 1989 protests in Shanghai is regarded as a masterclass in political survival. While Beijing chose military intervention, Jiang took a distinctly localized path: he balanced ideological firmness to please the hardliners with strategic, non-lethal de-escalation to prevent the crisis from spiraling. [](https://www.chinafile.com/library/nyrb-china-archive/jiang-zemin-mystery)

#### 1\. Clamping Down on the Media (To Please the Hardliners)

Before the street protests peaked, Jiang made a highly decisive move against the press. A prominent, liberal Shanghai newspaper called _The World Economic Herald_ planned to publish an issue praising the late reformer Hu Yaobang and validating the students' demands. 

Jiang intervened directly, ordering the paper to censor the articles. When the chief editor refused, Jiang completely **suspended the editor and shut down the paper**. This sent a clear shockwave through the intellectual community, proving to Deng Xiaoping and the Beijing conservatives that Jiang would fiercely defend party authority without hesitation. [](https://asia.nikkei.com/politics/xi-hails-jiang-zemin-s-opposition-to-tiananmen-protests)

#### 2\. Personal Diplomacy and Empathy (To Pacify Students)

Instead of hiding behind police lines or treating the students like enemies of the state, Jiang engaged with them directly. He went out into the streets to confront massive crowds of student protesters. [](https://www.chinafile.com/library/nyrb-china-archive/jiang-zemin-mystery)

Because of his pre-revolutionary elite education, he did something Beijing leaders refused to do: he spoke to them with respect. He famously told the crowd, **"Your patriotism and aspirations are admirable,"** and even used his fluent English to recite the Gettysburg Address to prove he understood and respected Western democratic concepts. This defused a massive amount of tension, making the students feel heard rather than attacked. [](https://www.chinafile.com/library/nyrb-china-archive/jiang-zemin-mystery)

#### 3\. Rejecting the Military (To Prevent Bloodshed)

As protests intensified after the declaration of martial law on May 20, Jiang made a critical operational decision: **he steadfastly refused to call armed military troops into Shanghai**. He explicitly stated that he did not want the military occupying his city. [](https://www.latimes.com/archives/la-xpm-1989-06-25-mn-6438-story.html)

Instead of soldiers with rifles, Jiang organized **"workers' pickets"**—unarmed factory worker squads tasked with keeping the peace, clearing barricades, and maintaining urban order. When a tragic accident occurred where a train plowed into demonstrators blocking tracks, it sparked riots; instead of a massacre, Jiang used the civil court system to swiftly try and execute the specific ringleaders accused of arson. Order was restored through law enforcement, not a military assault. [](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0920203X9000500101)

#### The Ultimate Prize

By the time the smoke cleared after June 4th, Jiang Zemin was one of the only major leaders whose reputation was pristine across factions. [](https://time.com/archive/6951853/what-really-happened/)

* To the **hardliners and elders**, he was a hero who had crushed a rebellious newspaper and backed martial law.
* To the **economic reformers and citizens**, he was a sensible manager who had kept Shanghai completely free of military bloodshed. [](https://www.chinafile.com/library/nyrb-china-archive/jiang-zemin-mystery)

This clean record made him the compromise candidate that Deng Xiaoping selected to lead the entire nation. [](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1989%5FTiananmen%5FSquare%5Fprotests%5Fand%5Fmassacre)
