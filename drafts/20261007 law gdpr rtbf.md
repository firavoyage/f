# what is the right to be forgotten

The **right to be forgotten** (RTBF) is a legal concept that allows individuals to request the permanent removal of their personal data or links to that data from the internet. Formally known as the **right to erasure**, it aims to give people control over their digital footprint so that past mistakes, outdated information, or private details do not follow them indefinitely. [link](https://gdpr.eu/right-to-be-forgotten/)

Here is a breakdown of how it works, its legal standing, and why it is highly debated:

### 🌍 Where It Applies

- **The European Union (EU):** The right was first established by the European Court of Justice in a landmark **2014 ruling against Google** (_Google Spain_). It was later codified under **Article 17 of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)**. Under the GDPR, EU residents can ask organizations or search engines to delete information or delist links that appear when searching for their names. [link](https://gdpr-info.eu/issues/right-to-be-forgotten/)
- **The United States:** U.S. courts **do not recognize a general right to be forgotten**, as it directly conflicts with the **First Amendment's protections for free speech and freedom of the press**. However, minor exceptions exist at the state level. For example, the [California Eraser Law](https://epic.org/state-right-to-be-forgotten-policy/) allows minors to remove content they previously posted online. [link](https://firstamendment.mtsu.edu/article/right-to-be-forgotten/)
- **Other Countries:** Nations like Argentina, South Korea, and various other jurisdictions have adopted or explored similar privacy laws modeled after the EU framework. [link](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right%5Fto%5Fbe%5Fforgotten)

### ⚖️ It Is Not Absolute

The right to be forgotten is a balancing act; you cannot simply erase everything you dislike about yourself online. Search engines and organizations evaluate requests based on specific criteria. [link](https://gdpr.eu/right-to-be-forgotten/)

| When It Applies ✅                                                                    | When It Is Denied ❌                                                                                                         |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| The data is **no longer necessary** for its original purpose.                         | Keeping the data is necessary to protect **freedom of expression and information**.                                          |
| You **withdraw your consent** (and the company has no other legal reason to keep it). | The information serves a vital **public interest** (e.g., public health, historical research, or a public figure's actions). |
| The data was **processed unlawfully** or is entirely inaccurate.                      | The organization is legally required to keep it (e.g., tax records, financial audits, or anti-money laundering laws).        |
| The data concerns **minor criminal history** or old, irrelevant debts.                | The data is required for **legal claims** or active law enforcement investigations.                                          |

### 🛠️ Deletion vs. Delisting

It is important to understand what happens when a request is approved by a search engine like Google. The search engine does not delete the article from the original website; it merely **delists (hides) the link** from showing up when someone searches for that specific individual's name. The information remains on the host website and can still be found through other search terms or direct links. [link](https://support.google.com/legal/answer/10769224?hl=en-GB\&gl=us)

### 🗣️ The Core Controversy

The right to be forgotten sits at a constant tension point between two fundamental rights:

1. **The Right to Privacy:** Proponents argue it protects people from cyberbullying, revenge porn, and being permanently penalized in their careers for minor, decades-old mistakes. [link](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right%5Fto%5Fbe%5Fforgotten)
2. **The Right to Free Expression:** Critics argue that allowing people to erase public information amounts to rewriting history, enables censorship, and limits the public's right to know the truth. [link](https://gdpr.eu/right-to-be-forgotten/)

# controversies on the right to be forgotten

The **"right to be forgotten" (RTBF)**, legally known as the **right to erasure** under the European Union’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), allows individuals to request that search engines and organizations remove outdated, irrelevant, or inaccurate personal information from internet searches. While championed as a major victory for personal digital privacy, it remains one of the most heavily debated concepts in modern internet law. [link](https://forum.nls.ac.in/ijlt-blog-post/the-right-to-be-forgotten-a-trial-of-controversy-and-conflict/)

The primary controversies surrounding the right to be forgotten stem from core legal, ethical, and practical conflicts:

### 1. Free Speech vs. Digital Privacy

The most significant conflict is between the **right to privacy** and the **right to freedom of expression and information**. [link](https://www.tcd.ie/news%5Fevents/articles/controversial-right-to-be-forgotten-law-discussed-at-adapt-public-lecture/)

- **The "Censorship" Argument:** Critics argue that allowing individuals to scrub truthful, previously public records (such as past news articles or legal notices) functions as a tool for historical revisionism and corporate or political censorship. [link](https://www.accessnow.org/eu-court-decides-on-two-major-right-to-be-forgotten-cases-there-are-no-winners-here/)
- **The Constitutional Divide:** This conflict creates a major geographical divide. In the United States, courts have repeatedly rejected a broad right to be forgotten, ruling in cases like _Garcia v. Google_ that forcing the removal of truthful information violates strict **First Amendment protections** against compelled speech and censorship. [link](https://www.stanfordlawreview.org/online/privacy-paradox-the-right-to-be-forgotten/)

### 2. Corporate Gatekeeping of Human Rights

Under current frameworks like the EU's, major private tech companies (such as Google or Bing) are the ones who initially receive and evaluate erasure requests. [link](https://www.financierworldwide.com/the-right-to-be-forgotten-by-internet-search-engines-real-right-or-real-controversy)

- Critics argue that shifting this burden onto a private corporation—rather than an independent court or public body—is inherently dangerous. [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YO0lbdhF30g\&t=140)
- Decisions regarding what is in the "public interest" versus what is "outdated" require nuanced ethical balancing. Tech companies may default to over-deleting valid information simply to avoid severe regulatory fines. [link](https://ideaexchange.uakron.edu/akronintellectualproperty/vol9/iss1/3/)

### 3. Public Accountability and Criminal Records

The application of RTBF to **past criminal records** is highly contentious. [link](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/VvSPgcFORvA)

- In some European jurisdictions, individuals who have served their sentences have successfully used the RTBF to remove links to news stories about their past crimes to aid their rehabilitation.
- However, public safety advocates argue that erasing this history restricts the public's right to know, limits transparency, and allows powerful individuals or repeat offenders to fully sanitize their digital backgrounds. [link](https://www.cima.ned.org/blog/balancing-act-newsworthiness-privacy-and-the-right-to-be-forgotten/)

### 4. Jurisdictional Creep and Global Applicability

Because the internet lacks physical borders, enforcing a regional law globally creates massive diplomatic and practical friction. [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YO0lbdhF30g\&t=140)

- Free-speech advocates worry about a "race to the bottom", where a restrictive privacy law or authoritarian regime in one country could force the global removal of content.
- To mitigate this, the European Court of Justice ruled that Google only has to delist links within European domains (like Google.fr or Google.de), meaning the information remains searchable on non-European domains (like Google.com). [link](https://www.nytimes.com/2015/08/06/technology/personaltech/right-to-be-forgotten-online-is-poised-to-spread.html)

### 5. Practical Implementation and Modern AI

Even when legally granted, the right to be forgotten is incredibly difficult to enforce technologically. Data is constantly mirrored, screenshotted, and re-shared across decentralized networks. Furthermore, the rise of **Generative AI and Large Language Models (LLMs)** poses a massive new challenge: once personal data is scraped and absorbed into an AI model's training weights, mathematically "erasing" that specific individual from the model's memory is incredibly complex. [link](https://www.weforum.org/stories/cybersecurity/eu-right-to-be-forgotten-online-data/)

# criticism on the right to be forgotten

The **"right to be forgotten"** (formalized under Article 17 of the EU’s General Data Protection Regulation as the "right to erasure") allows individuals to request that organizations or search engines delist or delete outdated, irrelevant, or excessive personal data. While viewed as a major victory for digital privacy, it has faced intense, multifaceted criticism from legal scholars, tech platforms, and human rights advocates. [link](https://sciendo.com/pdf/10.2478/iclr-2021-0015)

The primary criticisms span several distinct categories:

### 1. Free Speech and Censorship Concerns

The most prominent criticism—particularly from U.S. jurists and free expression advocates—is that the law acts as a mechanism for **privatized censorship**. [link](https://www.stanfordlawreview.org/online/privacy-paradox-the-right-to-be-forgotten/)

- **Rewriting History:** Critics argue that removing truthful, previously published information from search indexes effectively rewrites or whitewashes the public record. [link](https://jolt.law.harvard.edu/digest/balancing-privacy-and-speech-in-the-right-to-be-forgotten)
- **Conflict with Free Speech Laws:** In the United States, government-mandated data removal strongly conflicts with **First Amendment protections** for publishing truthful public records. [link](https://studentbriefs.law.gwu.edu/ilpb/2023/02/28/privacy-vs-free-speech-challenges-with-adopting-the-european-unions-right-to-be-forgotten-in-the-united-states/)
- **Chilling Effects:** Because tech companies face massive fines for non-compliance, critics argue they are incentivized to err on the side of caution and **default to deleting content**, choking off access to lawful information. [link](https://www.stanfordlawreview.org/online/privacy-paradox-the-right-to-be-forgotten/)

### 2. Corporate Gatekeeping and Lack of Due Process

The operational structure of the law effectively forces private tech corporations to act as judges. [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YO0lbdhF30g\&t=140)

- **Shifted Burden of Proof:** Search giants like Google are tasked with balancing an individual's right to privacy against the general public's right to know. Critics note that a private, profit-driven corporation is **not an appropriate forum** to arbitrate fundamental human rights. [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OnqyGkh7zIw\&t=16)
- **Lack of Transparency:** Delisting decisions are largely made behind closed doors by algorithms and corporate legal teams, bypassing traditional, transparent judicial systems. [link](https://reference-global.com/download/article/10.2478/iclr-2021-0015.pdf)

### 3. Practical and Technical Limitations

Opponents point out several severe logistical flaws that make the rule structurally inefficient. [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YO0lbdhF30g\&t=140)

- **The "Streisand Effect":** Public figures or individuals attempting to remove data often attract highly public legal battles, inadvertently drawing massive, renewed attention to the exact information they want hidden.
- **Geographical Fragmentation:** Because a state's jurisdiction is limited, a search result might be blocked on the European version of a search engine (e.g., Google.fr) but remains fully accessible on global domains (e.g., Google.com).
- **Data Backups:** Completely purging data across modern, distributed cloud systems and immutable backups presents severe, unresolved technical hurdles. [link](https://www.nytimes.com/2019/09/24/technology/europe-google-right-to-be-forgotten.html)

### 4. Erasure of Public Interest and Historical Records

Historians, librarians, journalists, and researchers heavily critique the law for its potential long-term damage to the collective human archive. [link](https://american-archivist.kglmeridian.com/view/journals/aarc/81/1/article-p100.xml)

- **Weaponization by Public Figures:** There is ongoing concern that politicians, criminals, or corrupt officials can exploit the law to hide past misdemeanors, bankruptcies, or controversial statements under the guise of "outdated information".
- **Damage to Academic Research:** Erasing structural data links limits the ability of sociologists, economists, and historians to accurately map past digital behaviors and trends. [link](https://www.stanfordlawreview.org/online/privacy-paradox-the-right-to-be-forgotten/)

# history of the right to be forgotten

The **right to be forgotten** (RTBF), legally known as the **right to erasure**, is a modern data privacy concept that allows individuals to request the deletion or removal of personal data from internet search results and databases. While it gained global fame in the digital age, its underlying legal philosophy stretches back decades. [link](https://gdpr.eu/right-to-be-forgotten/)

### 1. Pre-Digital Roots: _Le Droit à l’Oubli_

Long before the internet, European legal systems recognized concepts related to human memory and rehabilitation. [link](https://www.stanfordlawreview.org/online/privacy-paradox-the-right-to-be-forgotten/)

- **French Law:** The intellectual foundation stems from the French concept of **_le droit à l’oubli_** (the right of oblivion). This traditional right allowed rehabilitated criminals who served their sentences to object to the ongoing publication of their past crimes so they could reintegrate into society. [link](https://www.stanfordlawreview.org/online/privacy-paradox-the-right-to-be-forgotten/)
- **Early Data Protection (1995):** The EU formalized early iterations of data management in the [1995 Data Protection Directive](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/interface/2019/privacy-theres-more-to-it-than-gdpr/the-evolution-of-the-eus-right-to-be-forgotten). Article 12 of the directive granted citizens the right to ask data controllers to erase or block personal information if its processing did not comply with the law (e.g., if the data was inaccurate or incomplete). [link](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/interface/2019/privacy-theres-more-to-it-than-gdpr/the-evolution-of-the-eus-right-to-be-forgotten)

### 2. The 2012 Proposal

In January 2012, European Commission Vice-President Viviane Reding officially introduced a comprehensive draft for what would become the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). Reding explicitly advocated for a formalized "right to be forgotten," arguing that citizens—especially teenagers—needed a mechanism to erase digital footprints that lacked a legitimate reason for keeping. [link](https://review.law.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2012/02/64-SLRO-88.pdf)

### 3. The 2014 Landmark Case: _Google Spain_

The modern definition of the RTBF was solidified by the **Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU)** on **May 13, 2014**, in the landmark ruling _Google Spain SL v. Costeja González_. [link](https://ks.echr.coe.int/documents/d/echr-ks/right-to-be-forgotten)

- **The Conflict:** A Spanish citizen, Mario Costeja González, filed a complaint because search results for his name still linked to a 1998 newspaper notice about an auction of his property to pay off debts. Since the debt had long been resolved, he argued the links were no longer relevant. [link](https://firstamendment.mtsu.edu/article/right-to-be-forgotten/)
- **The Ruling:** The CJEU ruled in favor of González. It declared that search engines act as data controllers. Consequently, they must remove links to personal info upon request if the data is "inadequate, irrelevant or no longer relevant," balancing an individual’s privacy against the public's right to know. [link](https://www.varonis.com/blog/right-to-be-forgotten)

### 4. Codification in the GDPR (2018)

Following the _Google Spain_ precedent, the EU explicitly codified the rule under **Article 17 of the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)**, which went into effect in **May 2018**. Under Article 17, individuals can demand data erasure under specific conditions, such as when data is no longer necessary or consent is withdrawn. However, the right is **not absolute** and can be overridden by freedom of expression, public health interests, or legal obligations. [link](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/individual-rights/individual-rights/right-to-erasure/)

### 5. Territorial Expansion and Global Friction (2019)

A major historical battleground was whether the EU could force search engines to delete links globally. In **September 2019**, the CJEU ruled in _Google v. CNIL_ that Google was only obligated to delist search results within European domains (like Google.fr or Google.de) and use geo-blocking to prevent European users from seeing them, rather than scrubbing them worldwide. [link](https://www.clarip.com/data-privacy/right-forgotten-history/)

# high profile events on the right to be forgotten

The **"right to be forgotten"** (or the right to erasure) has been shaped by a series of landmark legal battles and policy updates, primarily involving global tech giants like **Google** and European regulators. These high-profile events highlight the ongoing tension between personal privacy and the public's right to information. [link](https://www.bbc.com/news/technology-49808208)

The defining events that have shaped this legal landscape include:

### 1. The Genesis: _Google Spain v. AEPD_ (2014)

This landmark case established the modern right to be forgotten. A Spanish citizen, Mario Costeja González, argued that Google search results for his name still linked to a 1998 newspaper article about his past debts, which had long been resolved. The [Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU)](https://support.google.com/legal/answer/10769224?hl=en\&gl=us) ruled in his favor, declaring that search engines are "data controllers" and must remove links to info that is "inadequate, irrelevant, or excessive" upon request. [link](https://support.google.com/legal/answer/10769224?hl=en\&gl=us)

### 2. Setting Borders: _Google v. CNIL_ (2019)

After the 2014 ruling, the French privacy regulator, CNIL, demanded that when Google approves a "right to be forgotten" request, it must remove those links from search results **globally**, not just on European domains. Google resisted, fearing censorship loops in authoritarian countries. In September 2019, the [CJEU handed Google a major victory](https://www.bbc.com/news/technology-49808208), ruling that the right to be forgotten **only applies within the European Union**. Google was required to use geoblocking to hide the links from European users but did not have to scrub worldwide search results. [link](https://jolt.law.harvard.edu/digest/google-v-cnil-eu-rules-that-right-to-be-forgotten-does-not-apply-globally)

### 3. Codification via GDPR (2018)

The right evolved from a court-ruling concept into formal legislation when the EU’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) took effect in 2018. Under **Article 17 (Right to Erasure)**, citizens gained explicit legal grounds to demand the deletion of their personal data from organizations under specific criteria. [link](https://www.clarip.com/data-privacy/right-forgotten-history/)

### 4. Shifting the Burden of Proof (2022)

In the case _TU, RE v. Google LLC_, the CJEU expanded the scope of Article 17 to include photographs and thumbnails. Crucially, the court ruled that if a user proves online data about them is **"manifestly inaccurate,"** the search engine must remove it. Users must submit sufficient evidence of the inaccuracy, but they do not need to obtain a prior judicial order to force the platform's hand. [link](https://www.reuters.com/technology/google-must-remove-data-search-results-if-user-proves-it-is-wrong-eu-top-court-2022-12-08/)
