# when can that in "noun that does sth" be omitted

The word "that" can **never** be omitted <mark>when it serves as the subject of the relative clause</mark>, which is the case in the structure "noun + that + does something."

### The Grammatical Rule

- In "noun that does something," the word "that" is a **subject relative pronoun**.
- It represents the noun performing the action ("does").
- English grammar requires subject relative pronouns to stay in the sentence to connect the noun to the verb.

### Examples of Incorrect Omission

- **Incorrect:** I know a guy works at Google.
- **Correct:** I know a guy **that** works at Google.
- **Incorrect:** The car blocked the driveway was red.
- **Correct:** The car **that** blocked the driveway was red.

### Exceptions: When You Can Omit "That"

You can only omit "that" if it is the **object** of the relative clause (the noun receives the action instead of doing it), or if you rewrite the sentence using a participle.

- **Object Relative Clause:** "The book **that** I read" can become "The book I read." (Because "I" is the subject, not "that").
- **Reduced Relative Clause (Using -ing):** You can remove "that" if you change the verb into its "-ing" form.
  - "The man **that lives** next door" becomes "The man **living** next door."
  - "The train **that leaves** at 5 PM" becomes "The train **leaving** at 5 PM."

# object relative clause examples

An **object relative clause** is a dependent clause that describes a noun in the main sentence, where the relative pronoun (like _that_, _which_, _who_, or _whom_) serves as the **direct or indirect object** of the action inside the clause. You can easily recognize an object relative clause because the relative pronoun is always followed by a new subject and a verb. [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YOGtzi8F07Y\&t=6)

Here is a breakdown of everyday examples, categorized by what they describe:

### Describing Things or Animals (_That_ / _Which_)

- **The laptop** \[that I bought last year] is already running slow.
  - _Analysis:_ "I" is the subject of the clause; "bought" is the verb; "that" (the laptop) is the object. [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YOGtzi8F07Y\&t=6)
- **The book** \[which Sarah recommended] was a massive bestseller.
- **The dog** \[that we adopted yesterday] loves to play fetch.

### Describing People (_Who_ / _Whom_ / _That_)

- **The teacher** \[who I met this morning] was incredibly helpful. [link](https://www.ef.edu/english-resources/english-grammar/defining-relative-clauses/)
- **The candidate** \[whom the board selected] has twenty years of experience.
  - _Note:_ _Whom_ is the grammatically precise object pronoun used in formal writing. [link](https://www.ef.edu/english-resources/english-grammar/defining-relative-clauses/)
- **The actor** \[that you recognized from the commercial] just won an award.

### Object of a Preposition

When the noun is the object of a preposition (like _with_, _to_, or _about_), the pronoun can sit at the beginning or end of the clause. [link](https://writingcenter.unc.edu/tips-and-tools/relative-clauses/)

- **Formal:** The client \[with whom he was negotiating] is the CEO.
- **Informal:** The client \[who he was negotiating with] is the CEO.
- **Formal:** This is the project \[to which they dedicated their lives].
- **Informal:** This is the project \[which they dedicated their lives to]. [link](https://www.ef.edu/english-resources/english-grammar/preposition-placement-relative-clauses/)

***

### Key Rules to Remember 💡

#### 1. You Can Omit the Pronoun

In defining relative clauses, if the relative pronoun functions as an **object**, you can drop it completely without changing the meaning. If it is a subject, you cannot drop it. [link](https://www.ef.edu/english-resources/english-grammar/defining-relative-clauses/)

- **With pronoun:** The movie **that** we watched last night was scary.
- **Dropped pronoun:** The movie \[ ] we watched last night was scary. _(Perfectly correct)_ [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yp2abLdBtg4\&t=4)

#### 2. Do Not Double the Object

Because the relative pronoun already stands in for the noun, you must never repeat the object pronoun (_it_, _him_, _her_, _them_) at the end of the relative clause. [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=YOGtzi8F07Y\&t=6)

- ❌ **Incorrect:** The cake \[that I baked **it**] was delicious.
- **Correct:** The cake \[that I baked] was delicious. [link](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/grammar/british-grammar/relative-clauses-defining-and-non-defining)

## when can i omit when not

You can omit the relative pronoun only <mark>when it acts as the<span> </span><strong>object</strong><span> </span>of the relative clause</mark>. You cannot omit it when it acts as the **subject**.

The easiest way to tell the difference is to look at what comes **immediately after** the relative pronoun.

### Omit: Followed by a Noun or Pronoun 🟢

If the word right after the relative pronoun is a **subject** (a noun or pronoun like _I, you, he, she, they, Sarah_), you can safely omit it.

- **With pronoun:** The phone **that** _you_ bought is on the table.
- **Omitted:** The phone \[ ] _you_ bought is on the table.
- **With pronoun:** The doctor **who** _I_ saw yesterday was very kind.
- **Omitted:** The doctor \[ ] _I_ saw yesterday was very kind.

### Do NOT Omit: Followed by a Verb 🔴

If the word right after the relative pronoun is a **verb** or an **auxiliary verb** (like _is, ran, wanted, has_), the pronoun is the subject. You **must keep it**.

- ❌ **Incorrect:** I know a man \[ ] _lives_ in Chicago.
- **Correct:** I know a man **who** _lives_ in Chicago.
- ❌ **Incorrect:** The car \[ ] _was_ stolen has been found.
- **Correct:** The car **that** _was_ stolen has been found.

***

### The One Big Exception: Non-Defining Clauses ⚠️

Even if the pronoun acts as an object, you **cannot** omit it if the clause is "non-defining." These are extra-information clauses that sit between **commas**.

- ❌ **Incorrect:** My favorite laptop, \[ ] I bought last year, is broken.
- **Correct:** My favorite laptop, **which** I bought last year, is broken.

## do ppl omit anyway in informal speaking

Yes, native speakers often drop the relative pronoun in informal speech, but **only in specific conversational patterns** that violate strict grammar rules.

Here is exactly how people break the rules in casual conversation:

### 1. The "There is / There's" Shortcut

In standard grammar, you cannot drop the subject pronoun. However, in casual spoken English, speakers almost always drop **that** or **who** after phrases like _There is_, _There are_, _It is_, or _Here is_.

- **Strict Grammar:** There’s a guy **who** wants to talk to you.
- **Informal Speech:** There’s a guy \[ ] wants to talk to you.
- **Strict Grammar:** Here is the package **that** arrived for you.
- **Informal Speech:** Here's the package \[ ] arrived for you.

### 2. Dropping Pronouns in "Cleft" Sentences

When emphasizing a specific person or thing using _"It was \[Noun]..."_, speakers routinely drop the pronoun in casual speech.

- **Strict Grammar:** It was Sarah **who** told me the secret.
- **Informal Speech:** It was Sarah \[ ] told me the secret.

### 3. Re-arranging Prepositions to Allow Omission

In formal speech, you cannot omit the pronoun if a preposition sits in front of it (_"The chair on which I sat"_). In informal speech, people move the preposition to the very end of the sentence specifically so they **can** drop the pronoun.

- **Formal (Cannot omit):** The hotel **at which** we stayed was dirty.
- **Informal (Omitted):** The hotel \[ ] we stayed **at** was dirty.

***

### When Speakers Still NEVER Omit

Even in the most casual slang or informal speech, native speakers will **never** drop the pronoun in a normal subject sentence because it ruins the flow of the sentence.

- ❌ **Nobody says:** I bought a car \[ ] broke down yesterday.
- **Everyone says:** I bought a car **that** broke down yesterday.
