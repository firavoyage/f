## why do you write assume instead of forall

In mathematical induction, we write **"assume"** instead of **"for all" (**

**∀**

**)** in the inductive hypothesis because of a fundamental logical rule: <mark><strong>you cannot use your final destination as a starting assumption.</strong></mark>

If you say _"assume this is true for all_

_𝑛_

_"_, you have instantly assumed the very thing you are trying to prove, which is a logical fallacy called **begging the question** (circular reasoning).

Here is the breakdown of why "assume" works and "for all" fails.

***

### 1. The Strategy of Induction (The Domino Analogy)

Think of mathematical induction like knocking down a line of dominoes:

1. **Base Case:** You prove you can knock down the very first domino (\
   𝑛\
   \=0\
   ).
2. **Inductive Step:** You prove that **if** any single domino falls, it has enough force to tip over the _next_ domino right after it (\
   𝑘\
   →\
   𝑘

+1\
).

When writing the Inductive Hypothesis, you are testing that **"if"** condition. You pick a single, specific, arbitrary domino out of the line—let's call it domino

𝑘

—and say: _"Let’s **assume** this specific domino falls. Can it reach domino_

_𝑘_

_+1_

?"

***

### 2. The Logic Shift

Notice the massive difference in scope between the two words:

- ❌ **Using "For All" (**

### 3. The Logical View: Global Laws vs. Local Proofs

People also split their usage based on whether they are **stating a theorem** or **executing a proof**.

- **"If" belongs in Theorems (Global):** When stating a global law, people almost always use "if/then" because it frames the permanent boundaries of the rule.

> _"**If** a sequence is monotonic and bounded, **then** it converges."_

- **"Assume" belongs in Proofs (Local):** When working inside the machinery of the proof, people shift to "assume" because they are actively building that specific reality.

> _"To prove this, **assume** the sequence is monotonic and bounded..."_

***

### Summary: The Structural Consensus

In the mathematical community, using "if" is seen as perfectly logical, but stylistically passive. **People think of "assume" as the superior narrative tool** because it allows the writer to anchor a hypothesis locally, untangle the grammar, and walk the reader through the mathematical deductions in active, straightforward steps.
