# prep question word sentence or question word sentence prep

Both structures are grammatically correct in English. The choice depends on the formality of the situation.

### Prep + Question Word + Sentence (Formal)

This structure is common in formal writing and academic contexts. Moving the preposition to the front is called "pied-piping."

- **Example:** **To whom** are you speaking?
- **Example:** **In which** house do they live?

### Question Word + Sentence + Prep (Informal)

This structure is preferred in everyday spoken English. Leaving the preposition at the end is called "preposition stranding."

- **Example:** **Who** are you speaking **to**?
- **Example:** **Which** house do they live **in**?

### Key Differences

- **Tone:** Fronted prepositions sound stiff or traditional. Stranded prepositions sound natural and casual.
- **Pronoun shifts:** When the preposition comes first, you must use **whom** instead of **who**. When it comes last, **who** is standard.

# compare prep question word sentence and question word sentence prep

The choice between starting a question with a preposition ("Prep + Question Word") or ending with it ("Question Word... + Prep") depends entirely on the level of formality required.

Here is the comparison between the two structures.

### **Key Differences**

- **Formal vs. Informal**: Preposition-first is highly formal. Preposition-last is casual and common.
- **Speaking vs. Writing**: People rarely speak with the preposition first. They use it in academic writing.
- **Pronoun Changes**: "Who" often changes to "whom" when it follows a preposition directly.

### **Structure Comparison**

#### **Prep + Question Word Sentence**

- **Tone**: Formal, academic, or literary.
- **Rules**: The preposition moves to the front, directly before the pronoun.
- **Example**: **To whom** were you speaking?
- **Example**: **In which** house do they live?

#### **Question Word Sentence + Prep**

- **Tone**: Informal, conversational, and natural.
- **Rules**: The preposition sits at the very end of the sentence.
- **Example**: **Who** were you speaking **to**?
- **Example**: **Which** house do they live **in**?

### **When to Use Each**

- **Use Preposition First**: Essays, business reports, legal documents, or formal speeches.
- **Use Preposition Last**: Everyday conversations, text messages, emails, and fiction dialogue.

# prep which clause or which clause prep

<mark><strong><span>Both options are grammatically correct</span></strong></mark>, but they belong to different levels of formality. [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Hk1an-Wx1cM)

- **Preposition + _Which_ Clause** is formal and typically used in academic, legal, or professional writing.
- **_Which_ Clause ... + Preposition** (also known as a "stranded preposition") is informal and used in natural, everyday conversation. [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Hk1an-Wx1cM)

***

### Comparison Matrix

| Structure                     | Formality      | Example                                | Note                              |
| ----------------------------- | -------------- | -------------------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| **Preposition + _Which_**     | High (Formal)  | "The house **in which** I live..."     | Excellent for essays and reports. |
| **_Which_ ... + Preposition** | Low (Informal) | "The house **which** I live **in**..." | Natural for everyday speech.      |

***

### Deep Dive & Examples

#### 1. Preposition + _Which_ (Formal)

In formal writing, the preposition is pulled to the very front of the relative clause, right before the word _which_. [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zvddzzLtTx0)

- "This is the table **under which** I left my bag."
- "The project **on which** we are working is confidential."
- "The era **during which** they lived was peaceful." [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zvddzzLtTx0)

_Grammar Tip:_ When the preposition is at the front, you **cannot** swap _which_ out for the word _that_ (e.g., "the table ~~under that~~ I left my bag" is incorrect). [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zvddzzLtTx0)

#### 2. _Which_ Clause + Preposition (Informal)

In casual speech, native speakers prefer to leave the preposition at the very end of the clause. [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zvddzzLtTx0)

- "This is the table **which** I left my bag **under**."
- "The project **which** we are working **on** is confidential." [link](https://aemacademy.ca/the-complex-sentence-system/relative-clause-type-3-preposition-plus-whom-or-which/)

_Grammar Tip:_ In this informal structure, you can drop the word _which_ entirely, or replace it with _that_. (e.g., "The project we are working on..." or "The project that we are working on..."). [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zvddzzLtTx0)

The primary difference between a **preposition + "which"** clause and a **"which" + preposition** clause is **formality and sentence structure**. Both structures express the exact same meaning, but they place the preposition in different positions. [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Hk1an-Wx1cM)

Here is a direct comparison of the two structures:

| Feature                   | Preposition + "Which" (`on which...`)                                  | "Which" + Preposition (`which... on`)                                       |
| ------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Formality**             | Highly formal; preferred in academic, legal, and professional writing. | Informal to neutral; standard in daily speech and casual writing.           |
| **Preposition Placement** | At the very beginning of the relative clause, right before "which".    | At the very end of the relative clause (known as a "stranded preposition"). |
| **Spoken English**        | Can sound rigid, stiff, or unnatural in casual conversation.           | Sounds completely natural and fluid to native speakers.                     |
| **Pronoun Omission**      | The word "which" **cannot** be omitted.                                | The word "which" **can** be omitted entirely in defining clauses.           |

***

### Structure 1: Preposition + "Which" (Formal)

In formal grammar, the preposition is moved to the front of the relative clause so it sits directly before the relative pronoun. This completely avoids ending the clause or sentence with a preposition. [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Hk1an-Wx1cM)

- **Pattern:** Noun + \[ **Preposition** + **Which** ] + Subject + Verb
- **Example 1:** This is the project **on which** we are working.
- **Example 2:** The candidate **with whom** they spoke was very impressive. _(Note: "Whom" is used for people instead of "which")_. [link](https://test-english.com/explanation/b2/relative-clauses-defining-non-defining/)

### Structure 2: "Which" + Preposition (Informal/Spoken)

In everyday English, people naturally leave the preposition attached to the verb at the end of the clause. This is called "preposition stranding." [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Hk1an-Wx1cM)

- **Pattern:** Noun + \[ **Which** ] + Subject + Verb + \[ **Preposition** ]
- **Example 1:** This is the project **which** we are working **on**.
- **Example 2:** This is the project we are working **on**. _(Omitting "which" entirely, which is very common in speech)_. [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Hk1an-Wx1cM)

### How to Choose the Right Preposition

The preposition you use is determined by the phrase or verb inside the clause, not by the word "which" itself. [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Hk1an-Wx1cM)

- If you say "listen **to** music," the clause becomes: _The music **to which** I listen_ OR \*The music **which** I listen **to\***.
- If you say "depend **on** a friend," the clause becomes: _The friend **on whom** I depend_ OR \*The friend **who** I depend **on\***.

# compare prep which clause and which clause prep

The primary difference between a **\[preposition + "which"]** clause and a **\["which" ... + preposition]** clause is <mark><strong>formality and sentence structure</strong></mark>. Both patterns convey the exact same meaning, but they place the preposition in different parts of the sentence. [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Hk1an-Wx1cM)

### Overview Comparison

| Feature       | Preposition + "Which" (Pied-Piping)                          | "Which" ... + Preposition (Preposition Stranding)          |
| ------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Structure** | The preposition sits **directly before** "which".            | The preposition is **left at the end** of the clause.      |
| **Formality** | Strictly **formal** (academic, legal, professional writing). | **Informal** to neutral (casual speech, everyday writing). |
| **Example**   | The project **on which** we are working...                   | The project **which** we are working **on**...             |
| **Omission**  | "Which" **cannot** be dropped from the sentence.             | "Which" **can often be dropped** in casual speech.         |

***

### 1. Preposition + "Which" Clause

In linguistics, this structure is known as **"pied-piping"** because the relative pronoun "which" pulls the preposition along with it to the very front of the relative clause.

- **Tone:** Highly academic, professional, and formal. [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Hk1an-Wx1cM)
- **Rule:** You must include "which". You cannot delete the relative pronoun when it immediately follows a preposition. [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Hk1an-Wx1cM)
- **Examples:**
  - This is the environment **in which** these organisms thrive.
  - The candidate **for whom** you voted has won. _(Note: "Whom" is used for people instead of "which" after a preposition)._ [link](https://blog.prepscholar.com/relative-pronouns-on-act-english-tips-and-practice)

### 2. "Which" Clause + Preposition

In linguistics, this structure is known as **"preposition stranding"** because the preposition is left behind ("stranded") at the very end of the modifier clause.

- **Tone:** Natural, conversational, and standard for everyday English. [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Hk1an-Wx1cM)
- **Rule:** If the relative pronoun is acting as the object of the clause, it can be entirely omitted in spoken English to make the sentence even shorter. [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Hk1an-Wx1cM)
- **Examples:**
  - This is the environment **which** these organisms thrive **in**.
  - This is the environment these organisms thrive **in**. _(Omitting "which" entirely for brevity)._ [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Hk1an-Wx1cM)

### Key Rules for Usage

- **Do not use "that" after a preposition:** While you can use "that" in stranded structures (_"The topic **that** I wrote **about**..."_), you can **never** place a preposition before "that" (_"The topic **about that** I wrote"_ is grammatically incorrect). [link](https://test-english.com/explanation/b2/relative-clauses-defining-non-defining/)
- **Replacing with adverbs:** In formal writing, some "preposition + which" combinations can be elegantly replaced by relative adverbs like **where** (instead of _in/at which_) or **when** (instead of _on/in which_).
  - _Formal:_ The house **in which** I grew up. → _Alternative:_ The house **where** I grew up. [link](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Hk1an-Wx1cM)
