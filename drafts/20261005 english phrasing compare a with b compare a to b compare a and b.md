The main difference is that **"compare A with B"** is traditionally used to look for differences between two similar things, while **"compare A to B"** is used to highlight similarities between two different things. However, **"compare A and B"** is a neutral phrase used when you want to examine both the similarities and the differences between two things equally.

In modern English, these distinctions have largely blurred, but understanding their nuanced definitions can help you choose the right phrasing for your context.

### The Differences at a Glance

| Phrase               | Primary Focus                                 | Best Used For...                                                                    | Example                                                               |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Compare A with B** | Discerning **differences**                    | Comparing two things of the same class or category to see how they differ.          | "The study **compared the health outcomes of Group A with Group B**." |
| **Compare A to B**   | Highlighting **similarities**                 | Drawing an analogy or noting a resemblance between two completely different things. | "Shall I **compare thee to a summer's day**?"                         |
| **Compare A and B**  | Finding **both** similarities and differences | Neutral analysis or instructions where you are looking at both items as a pair.     | "The essay prompt asks us to **compare the two political systems**."  |

### 1. Compare A with B (Focus on Differences)

When you compare something _with_ something else, you are typically placing two similar entities side-by-side to analyze their specific variations, metrics, or details. It implies a formal, analytical look at the contrast between two things of the same category.

- **Context:** Scientific studies, data analysis, product reviews, or financial reporting.
- **Example:** _"If you [compare Plan G from one company with Plan G from another](https://www.medicare.gov/publications/02110-choosing-a-medigap-policy.pdf), you will notice a stark difference in pricing."_ [link](https://www.medicare.gov/publications/02110-choosing-a-medigap-policy.pdf)

### 2. Compare A to B (Focus on Similarities)

When you compare something _to_ something else, you are usually declaring that one thing is like another. It is often used in metaphorical or literary contexts to illustrate a shared trait between otherwise unrelated concepts.

- **Context:** Analogies, idioms, creative writing, or casual observations.
- **Example:** _"The coach **compared the rookie's running style to** a young Barry Sanders."_

### 3. Compare A and B (Neutral Pairwise Comparison)

Using the conjunction _and_ treats both entities equally as the subject of the verb "compare." It does not bias the direction toward finding similarities or differences; it simply means you are evaluating the two items together.

- **Context:** General instructions, technical workflows, essay prompts, or operations (like [comparing two data columns in Excel](https://pitman-training.com/advice-hub/pitman-blog/how-to-compare-two-columns-in-excel-an-easy-guide/)).
- **Example:** _"The teacher asked us to **compare and contrast the book and the movie**."_ [link](https://pitman-training.com/advice-hub/pitman-blog/how-to-compare-two-columns-in-excel-an-easy-guide/)
